# Getting both price and extras: sequence, never bundle

When the goal is the lowest price *and* the most included, the instinct is to
ask for everything at once. That is the one approach guaranteed to get
neither.

## Why bundling fails

"I'll pay 309 000 if you include the service, the SOH report and transport"
converts the extras into currency. The seller now prices them, adds them to
the number, and you have paid for them. Worse, you have told them your real
constraint is total value rather than the headline figure, which is exactly
the flexibility they need to protect the price.

Every extra named while the price is still open becomes a reason for the
price not to move.

## The sequence that works

**Phase 1 — price only.** Anchor low, run the ladder, mention nothing you
want included. The walk-away threat is live and it is your only real leverage.
Any extra mentioned here weakens it.

**Phase 2 — settle.** Get the price agreed explicitly, in writing.

**Phase 3 — nibble.** *Now* add the extras, as conditions of signing today.
Small, individually trivial, framed as closing the deal rather than reopening
it.

> Flott, da er vi enige om [pris]. Jeg signerer i dag. Da regner jeg med at
> batterirapporten, en fersk service og full lading følger med?

## Why phase 3 works

The nibble is a small request made after the main terms are agreed, and it
works because the counterpart is already committed to the deal and does not
want to reopen it. Each item feels too minor to refuse against a
three-hundred-thousand-kroner transaction they have mentally banked.

Car dealers run this on buyers constantly: once the price is agreed they add
extended warranty, paint sealant, gap insurance, each "only 3 000 kr", and
recover a large share of the discount they just gave. The tactic works in
reverse and a dealer is in a poor position to complain about it.

Reported to work roughly six times in ten. Free.

## Rules for nibbling

- **Only after the price is agreed.** Before that it is a trade, not a nibble.
- **Ask for services, not money.** A service costs the dealer their internal
  cost, not retail, so their real outlay is a fraction of what it is worth
  to you. This is why it is easy for them to say yes.
- **One breath, several items.** Listing them together as an assumption
  ("da regner jeg med at ... følger med") is far stronger than three separate
  requests, each of which invites a separate no.
- **Frame as assumption, not request.** "Jeg regner med at" outperforms
  "kan dere også".
- **Stop after one round.** Nibbling twice reads as bad faith and can unwind
  a settled deal. Take what the first nibble yields.
- **Never nibble something that changes their economics materially.** Asking
  a dealer to eat a new set of tyres is not a nibble, it is a reopening.

## What is worth nibbling on a used EV

Ranked by value-to-you against cost-to-them:

| Item | Worth to you | Cost to dealer |
|---|---|---|
| Battery SOH report | 10 000-20 000 in risk | ~1 hour workshop time |
| Fresh service before handover | 3 000-6 000 | internal cost |
| Transport/delivery | 3 000-6 000 | a driver's afternoon |
| (drop this one if the buyer will collect anyway) | | |
| Second key | 3 000-5 000 | real cost, ask last |
| Full charge and all cables | small | nothing |
| Both tyre sets confirmed in contract | protects what you were promised | nothing |

The SOH report is the one to lead with. It costs them almost nothing and it
is the single most valuable document you can hold on a used EV.
